Simple round-trips are easy to book anywhere. Multi-city trips — fly into one city, out of another, with a couple of hops in between — are where flight search gets painful. Kiwi.com built its reputation on exactly these complex itineraries, stitching together flights (including separate tickets from different airlines) into bookable trips that traditional search engines won’t show you.

What Makes Kiwi.com Different for Complex Routes

Most flight search tools show you itineraries that airlines sell as single tickets. Kiwi.com goes further: its search combines flights across airlines that don’t partner with each other — including low-cost carriers — into one itinerary. According to their site, this “virtual interlining” can surface routes and prices that don’t exist as bookable tickets anywhere else.

For multi-city travel, this matters. A trip like New York → Lisbon, then Porto → Barcelona, then Barcelona → New York might price out awkwardly as separate bookings. Kiwi.com’s search is designed to treat the whole journey as one problem, mixing and matching carriers per leg. Their flexible search tools (date ranges, “anywhere” destinations) also help when your itinerary is still taking shape.

One thing to understand clearly: because some of these itineraries combine separate tickets, a missed connection isn’t always protected the way it is on a single airline ticket. Kiwi.com offers a guarantee product (check their site for the current terms) that covers rebooking in some disruption scenarios — read what it actually covers before relying on it, especially on tight connections.

Building a Good Multi-City Itinerary

Start with structure before price. Decide your open-jaw framework: which city you fly into, which you fly out of, and the order of stops in between. Open-jaw tickets (into A, out of B) usually beat backtracking, and Kiwi.com’s multi-city search handles this natively.

Then be strategic about connections. When Kiwi.com offers a self-transfer itinerary (separate tickets, you re-check bags and go through the connection yourself), leave generous layovers — three hours minimum, more for unfamiliar airports or international-to-domestic transfers. The savings on self-transfer routes are real, but only if the connection is actually makable. Short self-transfers are where complex itineraries go wrong.

Finally, sanity-check the total against booking legs separately. Sometimes Kiwi.com’s combined itinerary is genuinely cheaper; sometimes booking two simple tickets yourself costs the same with fewer complications. The platform is a tool, not an oracle — verify.

The Trade-Offs to Know

Honest downsides of booking complex itineraries through any online travel agency, Kiwi.com included. Changes and cancellations are harder than with the airline directly — modifications go through the agency, and separate-ticket itineraries can’t always be changed as a unit. Customer service during disruptions is the agency’s, not the airline’s, which matters when things go wrong at midnight in a foreign airport. Baggage on self-transfer itineraries means collecting and re-checking bags yourself, plus paying each carrier’s baggage fees separately.

None of this means “don’t use it” — it means go in with eyes open. For straightforward multi-city trips with comfortable layovers, Kiwi.com’s search is genuinely useful. For mission-critical tight connections, book directly with the airline.

The Bottom Line

Kiwi.com’s strength is finding and booking complex multi-city itineraries that traditional search misses, often at attractive prices. The trade-off is the usual OTA bargain: less flexibility and more complication if plans change. Build in generous connection times, understand what’s covered if things go wrong, and compare against booking legs separately. Prices change — verify at checkout.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is virtual interlining?

It’s Kiwi.com’s term for combining flights from airlines that don’t have ticketing partnerships — including separate tickets — into a single bookable itinerary. This can unlock cheaper or otherwise unavailable routes, but connections may be “self-transfer,” meaning you handle the connection yourself.

Is it safe to book separate-ticket itineraries?

It can be, with precautions: allow long layovers, understand that a missed connection isn’t automatically rebooked like a single-ticket itinerary, and check what Kiwi.com’s guarantee product covers. For tight or critical connections, book directly with the airline instead.

Can I check bags through on a Kiwi.com itinerary?

On single-ticket itineraries, usually yes. On self-transfer itineraries (separate tickets), generally no — you’ll collect and re-check bags at the connection, and each airline’s baggage fees apply separately. The booking should indicate which type you’re getting.

Is Kiwi.com cheaper than booking direct?

Sometimes — its search can surface combinations airlines don’t sell. Sometimes booking directly costs the same or less, with better change flexibility. Compare both for your specific route before committing.
