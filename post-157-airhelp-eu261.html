If your flight within, into, or out of Europe was delayed, cancelled, or overbooked, you may be owed real money under EU Regulation 261/2004 — not a voucher, not miles, but a cash payment. The catch: airlines don’t volunteer it, and the claims process is designed to wear you down. AirHelp is a service that pursues these claims on your behalf for a success fee. What EU261 Actually Entitles You To

EU261 is one of the strongest passenger-protection laws in the world. In broad strokes: if your flight was delayed by 3+ hours on arrival, cancelled, or you were denied boarding, and the cause was within the airline’s control (not extraordinary circumstances like severe weather or air traffic control strikes), you may be entitled to fixed compensation.

The statutory amounts are set by flight distance: up to €250 for short flights (up to 1,500 km), up to €400 for medium flights (1,500–3,500 km), and up to €600 for long flights (over 3,500 km). These are per passenger — a family of four on a qualifying long-haul delay could be looking at up to €2,400. Note the qualifiers: “up to,” “if your flight qualifies,” and “within the airline’s control.” Extraordinary circumstances exempt the airline, and the airline — not you — usually gets the first say on what counts, which is where disputes begin.

Eligibility also depends on the route and carrier: generally EU261 covers flights departing from the EU (any airline) and flights arriving in the EU on EU-based airlines. Similar schemes exist elsewhere — the UK has its own version post-Brexit — but the details differ, so check the rules for your specific itinerary.

What AirHelp Does

Filing an EU261 claim yourself means identifying the correct legal basis, writing to the airline, handling the rejection form letter, escalating to the national enforcement body, and possibly pursuing it further — in a foreign language, against a company with a legal department. Most people give up at the form letter. That’s the gap AirHelp fills.

According to their site, AirHelp checks your flight’s eligibility, files the claim with the airline, handles the back-and-forth, and escalates when the airline refuses — including legal action where they judge it worthwhile. You pay nothing upfront; they take a percentage of the compensation only if the claim succeeds. That success-fee model is the core proposition: no win, no fee.

The honest framing: you’re trading a share of the payout for not doing the work and for their experience with airline resistance tactics. Before You File: Gather This

Whether you use AirHelp or file yourself, your claim is only as strong as your documentation. Right now — before memories fade — collect: - Booking confirmation and boarding passes (screenshots are fine) - The actual arrival delay — EU261 runs on arrival time at the gate, not departure delay. Note when the doors opened. - The airline’s stated reason for the disruption, in writing if possible (gate announcements, emails, app notifications) - Receipts for any expenses the delay caused (meals, hotels) — these support separate reimbursement claims

One more thing: don’t accept a voucher as “compensation” at the airport without understanding what you’re signing. Airlines sometimes offer vouchers or miles on the spot that are worth less than the statutory amount — and accepting certain offers can complicate a later cash claim. When in doubt, take the documentation and decide later.

The Bottom Line

EU261 compensation is real money — up to €250/€400/€600 per passenger depending on flight distance — but only if your flight qualifies and only if someone pursues it past the airline’s first “no.” AirHelp’s success-fee model means you risk nothing upfront in exchange for a cut of the payout, which is a fair trade for travelers who’d rather not spend months arguing with an airline. Document everything now, check your eligibility, and don’t sign away your rights for an airport voucher. Frequently Asked Questions

How much compensation can I get under EU261?

The statutory amounts are up to €250 for flights up to 1,500 km, up to €400 for flights between 1,500 and 3,500 km, and up to €600 for flights over 3,500 km — per passenger, if your flight qualifies. The airline must have been responsible for the disruption; extraordinary circumstances like severe weather generally exempt them.

Does AirHelp charge upfront?

According to their site, no — they work on a success-fee basis, taking a percentage of the compensation only if your claim succeeds. Check the current fee percentage on their site before submitting, since it can vary by claim type and stage.

How long do I have to file a claim?

It depends on the country — limitation periods for EU261 claims vary by jurisdiction, typically ranging from about two to six years. Don’t sit on it indefinitely, though: evidence gets harder to gather with time, and some airlines are more cooperative with recent claims.

What if the airline already rejected my claim?

A rejection isn’t necessarily final — airlines reject many valid claims as a matter of routine. Services like AirHelp routinely take on previously rejected claims and escalate them, including through legal channels where appropriate. You can also escalate to your country’s national enforcement body yourself.
