Mattress Firmness Explained: How to Pick the Right Firmness for Your Sleep Position

Firmness is the single most misunderstood part of buying a mattress. Pick wrong and even an expensive, well-made mattress will leave you aching. Pick right and a mid-range mattress can feel perfect.

Here’s the problem: “firm,” “medium,” and “soft” mean different things to different brands. This guide gives you a universal 1-to-10 scale, tells you exactly which range suits your sleep position and body weight, and flags the mistakes that send thousands of mattresses back every year.

The 1-to-10 Firmness Scale

The mattress industry loosely grades firmness on a 10-point scale. It’s not standardized — one brand’s 6 might be another’s 7 — but it’s the best common language we have:

  • 1–2 (Ultra-soft): Practically a cloud. You sink deeply; almost no pushback. Rare in adult mattresses — mostly found in specialty or children’s products.
  • 3–4 (Soft): Deep contouring and sink. Shoulders and hips drop well into the mattress.
  • 5–6 (Medium-soft to medium): The most popular range. Noticeable contouring with enough support underneath to keep you from bottoming out.
  • 6–7 (Medium-firm): Gentle contouring on top, firm support below. The industry’s default “universal comfort” rating.
  • 8–9 (Firm): Very little sink. You sleep on the mattress, not in it. Strong pushback.
  • 10 (Extra-firm): Board-like. Almost no give. Only suits specific needs.

Most mattresses sold today fall between 4 and 8. If a product page just says “medium-firm” without a number, assume it’s around a 6.5 to 7.

Important: firmness is not the same as support. A soft mattress can still support you well if it keeps your spine aligned; a firm mattress can still let your hips sag if its support core is weak. Firmness is about feel. Support is about spinal alignment. You need both.

What Side Sleepers Need: Soft to Medium (3–6)

Side sleeping puts your full weight on two small areas — the shoulder and the hip. On a mattress that’s too firm, those pressure points take a beating: your shoulder jams upward, your spine curves sideways, and you wake up numb or aching.

Side sleepers generally need soft to medium firmness (about 3 to 6) so the mattress can cradle those pressure points and let the spine rest straight.

Fine-tune by body weight:

  • Under ~130 lbs: lean softer (3–5). Lighter bodies don’t compress firm mattresses enough to get pressure relief.
  • 130–230 lbs: the sweet spot is usually 5–6 — medium with enough give at the shoulder.
  • Over ~230 lbs: aim for 6–7 in a mattress with a strong support core. You still need pressure relief, but a too-soft surface will let your hips sink too far and twist your spine.

What Back Sleepers Need: Medium to Medium-Firm (5–7)

Back sleeping spreads your weight over the largest surface area, so pressure relief matters less. The priority is keeping your lower back supported — the mattress should fill the natural curve of your lumbar region without letting your hips sink.

Most back sleepers do best at medium to medium-firm (about 5 to 7).

  • Under ~130 lbs: 5–6 usually feels right.
  • 130–230 lbs: 6–7 is the classic back-sleeper range.
  • Over ~230 lbs: 7–8, with a genuinely supportive core (coils or high-density foam) so the midsection doesn’t hammock.

One useful test: slide your hand under your lower back while lying down. If there’s a big gap, the mattress is too firm for you. If your hand barely fits and your hips feel sunken, it’s too soft.

What Stomach Sleepers Need: Medium-Firm to Firm (7–9)

Stomach sleeping is the hardest position on a mattress — and honestly, on your body. Your midsection is the heaviest part of you, and on a soft surface it sinks while your head and legs stay higher, arching your lower back all night.

Stomach sleepers need medium-firm to firm (about 7 to 9) to keep the hips level with the rest of the body.

  • Most stomach sleepers land around 7–8.
  • Heavier stomach sleepers may need a full 8–9.
  • If you’re a stomach sleeper who also spends part of the night on your side, a 7 is usually the best compromise.

A pillow note: stomach sleepers should use a very thin pillow — or none at all — under the head, since a thick pillow cranks the neck backward.

What Combination Sleepers Need: Medium (5–7)

If you rotate through positions all night, you need a mattress that works acceptably everywhere rather than perfectly in one spot. Medium to medium-firm (5–7) is the safe zone, with responsiveness mattering as much as the number — you want a surface that’s easy to move on (hybrids and latex shine here; deep memory foam can feel like quicksand).

When in doubt, match the firmness to the position you fall asleep in, since that’s where you spend your longest uninterrupted stretch.

Body Weight Changes Everything

This is the factor most buyers ignore. The same mattress feels softer to a heavier person and firmer to a lighter one, because firmness is really about how far you sink in.

Body weight General guidance
Under ~130 lbs Go 1–2 points softer than the standard recommendation for your position
130–230 lbs Standard recommendations apply as written
Over ~230 lbs Go 1–2 points firmer, and prioritize a strong support core (coils or high-density base foam, 1.8+ lb density) over the comfort rating alone

Heavier sleepers should also pay attention to thickness — a mattress under 10 inches often doesn’t have enough material to support higher body weight without bottoming out. Twelve inches or more is the safer bet.

5 Common Firmness Mistakes

1. Buying firmer than you need “for your back.” The old advice that a hard mattress fixes back pain is outdated. Studies consistently show medium-firm — not firm — produces the best outcomes for back pain. Too-firm mattresses create pressure points that cause their own aches.

2. Trusting the showroom test. Lying on a mattress for 90 seconds in a store tells you almost nothing. Your body needs 20–30 minutes to settle, and one night isn’t enough either — most sleep experts say it takes 2 to 4 weeks to truly adjust. This is why trial periods matter so much.

3. Ignoring your partner’s needs. Couples with different positions or weights often compromise at a 6–7, or look at split-firmness options. Two people forcing themselves onto the wrong firmness is a recipe for both sleeping badly.

4. Confusing “plush top” with “soft mattress.” Many firm mattresses have a plush pillow top. The surface feels soft for the first inch, but the support underneath is firm. That’s often ideal — cushioning plus support — but don’t mistake it for an actually soft mattress.

5. Assuming firmness never changes. Foam softens with use — typically 10–15% in the first months, which engineers call “break-in.” A mattress that feels perfect on night one may feel a touch softer by month three. If you’re between two firmness levels, the slightly firmer option often ages better.

How to Test Firmness Before You Commit

  • Use the trial period. Most online brands offer 100+ night trials. Actually use it — don’t decide in week one.
  • Check the return terms before buying. Who pays return shipping? Is there a mandatory break-in period (often 30 days) before you can return? Is the refund full?
  • Read reviews filtered by your stats. Look for reviewers who mention their sleep position and body weight — their experience predicts yours far better than a star average.
  • Consider a topper as a fine-tuner. A 2–3 inch topper can soften a too-firm mattress by roughly a full point on the scale. It can’t fix a mattress that’s too soft, though — toppers only add softness, never support.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most popular mattress firmness?

Medium-firm, around 6 to 7 on the 10-point scale. It suits the widest range of sleepers — back sleepers, combination sleepers, and average-weight side sleepers — which is why most brands default to it.

Is a firm mattress better for back pain?

Medium-firm beats both extremes for back pain in most research. Too soft lets the spine sag; too firm creates pressure points at the shoulders, hips, and lower back. If back pain is your main concern, start at medium-firm and adjust from there.

How long does it take to get used to a new mattress firmness?

Plan on 2 to 4 weeks of break-in for both the materials and your body. Mild soreness in the first week is common and usually fades. If pain is getting worse after three weeks, the firmness is probably wrong.

Can two people with different firmness needs share a mattress?

Yes. Options include compromising on a medium (6–7), buying a split-firmness mattress (each side built differently — common in king sizes), or using two twin XL mattresses pushed together on a king frame. Adjustable air beds are another route, though they have their own tradeoffs.

Does mattress firmness change over time?

Yes — foam comfort layers soften with use, typically most in the first few months, then gradually over years. Coil cores hold their feel longer. Rotating the mattress every few months (if the design allows) evens out wear.
