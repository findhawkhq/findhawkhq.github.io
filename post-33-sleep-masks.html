Sleep Masks: How to Pick One That Doesn't Hurt (and Actually Blocks Light)

2026-10-13

A sleep mask is the cheapest blackout solution ever made. No installation, no measuring windows, works in hotel rooms and on red-eyes. But anyone who’s tried a bad one knows the failure modes: elastic that digs into your temples, fabric pressed against your eyeballs, and a glowing stripe of light across the nose bridge that defeats the entire purpose.

Here’s how to pick one that you’ll actually wear.

The Short Version

  • Contoured beats flat: a mask with molded cups around the eyes doesn’t press on your eyelids — more comfortable, and you can blink freely.
  • The nose bridge is where light leaks: this is the #1 design problem. Look for a shaped nose area or adjustable fit, not just a flat band of fabric.
  • Strap matters as much as the mask: wide, adjustable, soft straps don’t cause morning headaches. Thin elastic does.
  • Side sleepers need a slim profile: bulky masks get shoved around when your face is in the pillow.
  • Fit beats fabric: a well-fitting $15 mask outperforms a poorly fitting $60 silk one. Get the shape right first.

Why Bother With a Mask

Light suppresses melatonin even through closed eyelids, and most bedrooms aren’t as dark as people think — LEDs on chargers, hallway light under the door, streetlights, a partner reading. When you can’t control the room (travel, rentals, shift work, a partner on a different schedule), a mask is the portable fix. Pair it with blackout curtains at home and you’re covered everywhere.

Contoured vs. Flat

Flat masks are a single layer of fabric with elastic. They’re cheap and pack flat, but they press directly on your eyes — uncomfortable for most people over a full night, and a problem if you have long eyelashes or lash extensions.

Contoured (cupped) masks have molded eye cavities, usually foam or molded fabric. Your eyes sit in little dark caves with nothing touching them. You can open your eyes and blink inside the mask. For nightly use, this isn’t a luxury — it’s the difference between wearing it and abandoning it in a drawer.

If you’ve tried a mask before and hated it, you almost certainly tried a flat one. Try contoured before concluding masks aren’t for you.

What to Look For

Nose coverage. Hold the mask up and look at the nose area — a flat edge across the bridge guarantees a light stripe. Contoured masks usually shape around the nose; some have an adjustable nose wire (like a face mask). This single detail determines whether the mask actually works.

Wide, adjustable strap. Thin elastic straps create pressure points behind the ears and across the back of the head — the classic “mask headache.” Wide straps distribute pressure. Velcro or slider adjustment lets you get it snug without strangling.

Breathable, washable fabric. Cotton and silk are the common choices. Silk feels cooler and is gentler on skin; cotton is easier to wash and cheaper. Either way, wash it regularly — it sits on your face collecting skin oils for 8 hours a night.

Slim profile for side sleepers. If you sleep on your side, a thick foam mask gets pushed sideways and breaks its seal. Look for low-profile contoured designs, and accept some trial and error.

No pressure on the eyes. This is worth repeating because it’s the whole game. If you feel the fabric on your eyelids when you blink, it’s the wrong mask.

Masks and Eyelashes

Long natural lashes or extensions plus a flat mask equals bent, tangled lashes by morning. Deep-cupped contoured masks solve this completely — there’s simply clearance. If lashes matter to you, cup depth is the spec to check.

The Honest Take

Sleep masks have one of the best cost-to-benefit ratios in all of sleep products: a few dollars to remove light from the equation anywhere on earth. The entire trick is fit — contoured cups, a strap that doesn’t hurt, and no light at the nose. Get those three right and you’ll wonder why you waited.

The best sleep mask is the one you forget you’re wearing by the second night.
