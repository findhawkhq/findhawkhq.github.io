King vs Queen vs Cal King: Which Mattress Size Should You Actually Buy?

Mattress shopping has a size problem: most people buy the size they think they should want, not the size that fits their room, their sleep habits, and their budget.

Mattress shopping has a size problem: most people buy the size they think they should want, not the size that fits their room, their sleep habits, and their budget. A king in a 10×10 bedroom is a mistake. A couple squeezed onto a full is a slower, more expensive mistake — paid in bad sleep.

This guide lays out the actual dimensions, the room-size math, who each size suits, and the price and practical trade-offs nobody mentions until it’s too late.

The Short Version

Size Dimensions Fits Best for
Twin / Twin XL 38” × 75” / 80” Kids, single adults, bunk beds Kids, teens, guest rooms, small spaces
Full / Double 54” × 75” Tight for two Single adults who want space; couples only in small rooms
Queen 60” × 80” Most master bedrooms Couples (the default choice), tall singles
King 76” × 80” Large bedrooms (12’ × 12’+) Couples who want space, families with kids/pets in bed
Cal King 72” × 84” Large bedrooms, tall sleepers Tall sleepers (6‘2”+), West Coast standard

The one-line advice: Buy the biggest size that fits your room with walking space left over. Almost nobody regrets going bigger; plenty regret going smaller.

Actual Dimensions (US Standard)

Mattress sizes aren’t perfectly standardized — there’s typically ±1” of variation between brands — but the nominal dimensions are:

  • Twin: 38” × 75” — the kids’ and bunk bed standard.
  • Twin XL: 38” × 80” — 5 extra inches of length; the dorm room standard and the two halves of a split king.
  • Full (Double): 54” × 75” — 16” wider than a twin, same length.
  • Queen: 60” × 80” — the most popular size in the US by a wide margin.
  • Olympic Queen: 66” × 80” — 6” wider than standard; harder to find bedding for.
  • King: 76” × 80” — essentially two twin XLs side by side.
  • Cal King (California King): 72” × 84” — 4” narrower but 4” longer than a standard king.
  • Split King: two 38” × 80” twin XLs — the adjustable-base-friendly king.

The Room-Size Math

This is where most size mistakes happen. A mattress doesn’t exist alone — it needs clearance around it.

The rule: leave at least 24 inches of walking space on each side and at the foot of the bed. 30”+ is comfortable; under 24” feels cramped and makes changing sheets miserable.

Do the math for your room: - A queen (60” × 80”) in a 10’ × 12’ room: 120” − 60” = 60” of total side clearance → 30” per side. Comfortable. - A king (76” × 80”) in that same room: 120” − 76” = 44” → 22” per side. Tight — workable, but sheet-changing will annoy you. - A king needs a 12’ × 12’ room minimum to breathe; 13’ × 13’+ is genuinely comfortable.

Don’t forget: - Door swing — measure with the door open. - Dresser/nightstands — they eat wall space too. - Headboard and frame add 2–4” beyond the mattress dimensions. - Delivery path — a king won’t bend around a tight staircase corner. Measure hallways and turns (one more reason bed-in-a-box is convenient).

Queen vs King: The Real Decision

For couples, this is the decision. Here’s how to think about it:

Each person gets: - Full: 27” per person (narrower than a crib mattress is wide — genuinely cramped for two adults) - Queen: 30” per person - King: 38” per person

That 8” per person jump from queen to king is enormous in practice — it’s the difference between elbows touching all night and actual personal space. For couples dealing with motion disturbance, the king upgrade often helps more than a material change.

Choose the king if: your room fits it (12’ × 12’+), either of you is a sprawler, kids or pets join the bed, or one of you is a light sleeper.

Choose the queen if: the room is under 12’ × 12’, budget is tight (kings cost more — mattress, bedding, and frame all scale up), or you move frequently (queens are far easier to move).

The budget angle: a king mattress typically costs a few hundred more than the same model in queen — and king bedding, frames, and protectors all cost more too. The total cost of the size jump is bigger than the mattress price difference alone. (See How to Buy a Great Mattress on a Budget for keeping the whole purchase in check.)

Cal King: Who Actually Needs It?

The California king is 4” narrower and 4” longer than a standard king. That trade only makes sense for one group: tall sleepers.

  • If you’re 6‘2” or taller and your feet hang off a standard 80” mattress, those extra 4 inches matter.
  • If you’re under 6’, the Cal king gives up width you’d actually use for length you don’t need.
  • Bedding is harder to find for Cal king — fewer options, higher prices, less sale availability. This is a real ongoing cost.
  • It’s a regional standard — much more common on the West Coast, which is where the name comes from.

Unless you’re tall, buy the standard king.

Split King: The Adjustable-Base Answer

A split king is two twin XL mattresses (38” × 80” each) on a king frame. It’s the standard pairing for adjustable bases — each side articulates independently, so one partner can sit up reading while the other lies flat.

Considerations: - The middle gap. Two mattresses = a seam down the center. Good split kings minimize it; cheap setups have a noticeable valley. A king-size topper or a “bed bridge” pad solves it for most people. - Bedding: standard king sheets won’t fit two separate mattresses well — you need split-king sheet sets (two twin XL fitted sheets + king flat/top). - Cost: two twin XLs often cost slightly more than one king, plus the adjustable base itself.

For couples with very different firmness needs, a split king also enables split firmness — each side built to its sleeper. It’s the ultimate “stop compromising” configuration.

Singles, Kids, and Guest Rooms

  • Kids: Twin is the standard until the teen years; twin XL if they’re tall or you want the bed to last into adulthood. See Best Mattress for Kids.
  • Single adults: A full is the value sweet spot — much cheaper than a queen, plenty of room for one. A queen if you like space or the room allows it.
  • Guest rooms: Queen is the versatile choice — comfortable for couples visiting, not absurd for one guest. Full if the room is small.

Bedding Costs by Size: The Hidden Price of Going Bigger

The mattress price difference between sizes is only the beginning. Everything that touches the bed scales up:

Item Queen → King typical jump
Mattress A few hundred dollars more for the same model
Sheet sets $20–$60 more per set
Comforter/duvet $30–$80 more
Mattress protector $15–$40 more
Bed frame $50–$200+ more
Duvet cover $20–$50 more

Total hidden cost of queen → king: easily $150–$400+ beyond the mattress itself — and you’ll rebuy sheets and protectors every few years at the higher price.

Where it bites hardest: Cal king and split king bedding have fewer options and shallower discounts. Sale season (holiday weekends) narrows the gap, but off-season, odd sizes pay full price more often.

Budget strategy: If the king strains the budget, buy the king mattress but economize on the frame (a simple platform frame works as well as an expensive one for support) and build the bedding collection gradually. The sleep surface is the investment; the headboard is furniture.

Mattress Size FAQ

Will a king fit up my stairs / around the corner? Maybe not — and this surprises people constantly. A king is 76” × 80” of rigid rectangle. Measure every hallway, doorway, and stair turn on the delivery path before ordering. Bed-in-a-box kings ship compressed in a box (~2’ × 2’ × 4’), which solves the problem entirely — one more practical advantage of the online channel.

Is a California king good for shorter sleepers? No. The Cal king trades 4” of width for 4” of length versus a standard king. Under about 6‘2”, you won’t use the length and you’ll miss the width every night. Shorter couples should buy the standard king.

Can I use queen sheets on a full mattress? They’ll technically fit, but loosely — expect bunching and corners that pop off. Deep-pocket queen sheets on a standard full are especially sloppy. Buy the right size; ill-fitting sheets create wrinkles that bother light sleepers more than they’d expect.

The Bottom Line

Size mistakes are expensive and long-lasting — you’re stuck with the wrong size for a decade. Measure your room, leave real walking space, and buy the biggest size that fits comfortably. For couples on the fence between queen and king: if the room allows it, the king’s extra 8 inches per person is the single biggest sleep-quality upgrade most couples can buy.
