Pocketed Coils vs Bonnell Coils: Which Innerspring Is Worth Your Money?

Open up two innerspring mattresses and you’ll find two completely different machines inside. One uses an old interconnected wire system from the 1800s; the other uses hundreds of individually wrapped springs that move on their own. The difference in how they sleep — motion transfer, noise, pressure relief — is enormous, and it’s the main thing separating a $300 innerspring from an $800 one.

This guide explains both coil systems in plain English: how they’re built, how they feel, who each suits, and when the upgrade to pocketed coils is actually worth paying for.

What Are Bonnell Coils?

Bonnell coils are the original innerspring system — hourglass-shaped steel springs, wider at the top and bottom and narrower in the middle, all wired together into a single connected unit with spiral helical wires. When you press on one part of the mattress, the connected springs around it move too. It’s one big springy grid.

This is the coil system in most budget innersprings and old-school mattresses. It’s cheap to manufacture, which is why it survives: it keeps mattress prices down.

How Bonnell coils feel

  • Bouncy and firm. The interconnected design pushes back as a unit — a classic, springy, “on top of the bed” feel.
  • Lots of motion transfer. Your partner rolls over, and the whole grid ripples. Light sleepers feel everything.
  • Can be noisy. Metal-on-metal connections can squeak and creak, especially as the mattress ages.
  • Less contouring. Because springs move together, the system can’t follow your body’s curves precisely — pressure relief is the weakest of any coil type.

Bonnell coil pros

  • Cheapest coil system to make — the reason budget innersprings exist
  • Firm, supportive feel that some back and stomach sleepers genuinely prefer
  • Maximum airflow — the open structure breathes extremely well

Bonnell coil cons

  • Worst motion isolation of any modern coil system
  • Weakest pressure relief — rough for side sleepers
  • Tends to wear unevenly and get noisy over time
  • Shorter comfortable lifespan, typically 3 to 5 years in daily use

What Are Pocketed Coils?

Pocketed coils (also called Marshall coils) are individually wrapped springs — each coil sealed in its own fabric pocket, standing independent of its neighbors. Press down in one spot and only those springs compress; the rest stay put.

This is the coil system in most mid-range and premium innersprings and in nearly every hybrid mattress. It costs more to manufacture, but it fixes almost everything Bonnell gets wrong.

How pocketed coils feel

  • Responsive but independent. Still bouncy, but movement stays local — much closer to the “zoned” feel of a good hybrid.
  • Far less motion transfer. A partner moving on the other side barely registers. Not as isolating as memory foam, but dramatically better than Bonnell.
  • Better contouring. Independent springs follow shoulders and hips individually, so pressure relief is meaningfully better.
  • Quieter. Fabric pockets mean no metal-on-metal contact — no squeaking.

Pocketed coil pros

  • Best motion isolation of any coil system
  • Better pressure relief and spinal alignment
  • Quiet, even after years of use
  • Longer lifespan — quality pocketed systems hold up 7 to 10 years

Pocketed coil cons

  • More expensive — typically adds a few hundred dollars to the price versus Bonnell
  • Slightly less airflow than open Bonnell grids (still far better than foam)
  • Edge support varies — cheap pocketed units can feel weak at the sides unless reinforced

Coil Count and Gauge: The Two Numbers Worth Checking

Beyond coil type, two specs tell you about quality:

Coil count. More coils generally means more precise support — but only within the same coil type, and only up to a point. As a rough benchmark, around 800 or more pocketed coils in a queen is a healthy number; below 600, be skeptical. Above 1,000, you’re often paying for marketing rather than meaningfully better support.

Coil gauge. This measures wire thickness, and it runs backwards: a lower gauge number means thicker, firmer wire. Typical mattress coils run 12 to 15 gauge — 13 to 14 is the common middle ground. Thicker (lower gauge) coils feel firmer and hold up better under heavier bodies; thinner (higher gauge) coils feel softer and contour more.

Neither number matters as much as the coil type, but if you’re comparing two pocketed-coil mattresses, count and gauge are how you tell which one is built better.

The Other Coil Types (Briefly)

You’ll occasionally see two more systems worth knowing about:

  • Continuous coils. A single wire woven into rows of S-shaped coils. Cheap like Bonnell, slightly better motion isolation, but still one connected unit. Found in budget mattresses.
  • Offset coils. Hourglass coils like Bonnell but with squared-off tops that hinge, giving better contouring. A genuine middle ground — better than Bonnell, not as good as pocketed.

Neither changes the verdict below; they’re just names to recognize on spec sheets.

Which Is Worth Your Money?

Buy pocketed coils if:

  • You share the bed — the motion isolation difference is night and day
  • You’re a side sleeper who needs pressure relief
  • This is your every-night mattress for the next 5+ years
  • You’re a light sleeper, or your partner is restless

Bonnell is defensible if:

  • Budget is the absolute priority and you need the cheapest real mattress available
  • You sleep alone and don’t care about motion transfer
  • It’s for a guest room or kid’s room with light use
  • You specifically love a very firm, very bouncy traditional feel

The honest math: the price gap between a Bonnell and a pocketed-coil mattress is usually a few hundred dollars, and the pocketed version lasts roughly twice as long while sleeping better the entire time. Per year of use, pocketed coils are usually the cheaper option. Bonnell only wins when the upfront price is truly all that matters.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are pocketed coils better than memory foam for motion isolation?

No — memory foam still wins at motion isolation. But pocketed coils are the best coil option by a wide margin, and in a hybrid with foam comfort layers on top, the combination gets close to all-foam performance.

Do pocketed coil mattresses sag?

Less than Bonnell systems, but the comfort layers above the coils usually wear out before the coils do. In most sagging innersprings, it’s the foam and fiber on top that’s compressed, not the springs themselves. This is another reason foam quality matters even in a coil mattress.

How long do Bonnell coil mattresses last?

Typically 3 to 5 years of nightly use before comfort drops noticeably — and the decline is often noise and uneven wear, not just softening. For a primary bed, that’s a short lifespan.

Is a higher coil count always better?

No. Coil type matters more than count, and past roughly 1,000 pocketed coils in a queen, extra coils add little. A 900-coil unit with good gauge wire beats a 1,200-coil unit with thin wire.

Can you flip a coil mattress to make it last longer?

Only if it’s double-sided (finished on both sides), which is rare today. Most modern mattresses are single-sided — “no-flip.” Rotating head-to-foot every few months is the maintenance move that actually helps.
