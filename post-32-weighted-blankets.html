Weighted Blankets: The Honest Guide (Weight, Benefits, and Who Should Skip Them)

2026-10-12

Weighted blankets went from occupational therapy tool to mainstream sleep product in just a few years, carried by a simple promise: that gentle, even pressure calms your nervous system and helps you sleep. The marketing got ahead of the science — but there’s more here than hype.

Here’s what weighted blankets plausibly do, how to pick the right weight, and who should think twice.

The Short Version

  • The idea is “deep pressure”: evenly distributed weight is thought to activate the calming branch of your nervous system — similar to why swaddling soothes babies or a hug calms you down.
  • Evidence is modest: small studies link weighted blankets to reduced anxiety and better self-reported sleep. Evidence that they treat insomnia is thin. They’re a comfort tool, not a medical treatment.
  • The rule of thumb is ~10% of body weight: but comfort beats the formula — too heavy causes overheating and restlessness, which defeats the purpose.
  • Some people should skip them: young children, anyone with breathing difficulties or sleep apnea, people with claustrophobia, and anyone who can’t easily push the blanket off themselves.
  • Overheating is the #1 complaint: most of the weight comes from dense fill, which traps heat. Hot sleepers should prioritize breathable fabrics.

What They Might Actually Do

The proposed mechanism is deep pressure stimulation: sustained, distributed pressure that nudges the autonomic nervous system toward its “rest and digest” mode. Occupational therapists have used weighted blankets for years with anxious patients, and the logic translates plausibly to bedtime restlessness — that wired-but-tired feeling where your body won’t settle.

What the research shows so far: several small trials report lower anxiety and modestly better sleep quality with weighted blankets versus regular ones. What’s missing: large, rigorous trials showing they treat clinical insomnia. The honest summary is “probably helps you feel calmer, might help you sleep, definitely not a cure for a sleep disorder.”

Picking the Right Weight

The standard guidance is around 10% of your body weight — so roughly 15 pounds for a 150-pound person. Treat this as a starting point, not a prescription:

  • When in doubt, go lighter. A blanket that’s slightly too light still feels cozy. One that’s too heavy feels like being pinned down, and you’ll overheat.
  • Consider your build, not just the scale. Petite people often prefer less than the formula suggests.
  • Couples: one blanket sized for the lighter partner, or two separate blankets. A blanket heavy enough for a 220-pound person will bury a 130-pound one.
  • Kids: consult a pediatrician. The 10% rule is sometimes cited for children, but weighted blankets are not recommended for very young children, and a child should always be able to move the blanket off themselves.

Who Should Skip Them (or Ask a Doctor First)

  • Infants and toddlers: suffocation risk. Weighted blankets are not for very young children, period.
  • Sleep apnea or breathing difficulties: added weight on the chest is the wrong direction.
  • Claustrophobia: being held down is calming for some and panic-inducing for others. Know which camp you’re in.
  • Acid reflux: pressure on the abdomen can make nighttime reflux worse.
  • Anyone with limited mobility: if you can’t easily push it off or adjust it in the night, it’s a hazard, not a comfort.

What to Look For

Even weight distribution. The fill should be quilted into small pockets so it doesn’t all slide to your feet by 2 AM. This is the single most important construction detail.

Breathable outer fabric. Cotton or bamboo covers sleep cooler than synthetics. If you run hot, this matters more than the weight choice.

Removable, washable cover. A 20-pound blanket you can’t wash becomes unpleasant fast. A duvet-style removable cover solves this.

Glass beads over plastic pellets. Glass micro-beads are denser, quieter, and distribute more evenly. Most quality blankets use them now.

Reasonable size. A weighted blanket should cover you, not the whole bed — extra fabric hanging off the sides just slides off and drags.

The Honest Take

A weighted blanket is a reasonable thing to try if you lie awake feeling restless or anxious — the deep-pressure effect is plausible, the risk is low for healthy adults, and plenty of people swear by them. Just don’t buy one expecting it to fix insomnia, sleep apnea, or a mattress that’s past its prime. And if you sleep hot, solve the heat problem first (see our bedroom temperature guide), because no amount of calming pressure overcomes being sweaty.

Cozy is a legitimate sleep strategy. Just make sure “cozy” doesn’t mean “trapped and overheating.”
