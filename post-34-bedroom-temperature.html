Bedroom Temperature for Sleep: What the Research Actually Says

2026-10-13

Ask a sleep researcher for their single most underrated sleep tip and there’s a good chance the answer is “cool your bedroom.” Temperature is one of the few sleep factors with genuinely robust science behind it — and one of the easiest to fix.

Here’s what’s happening in your body, what temperature range the evidence points to, and how to get there without rebuilding your HVAC.

The Short Version

  • Your core temperature has to drop to fall asleep: about 1–2°F. A warm bedroom fights this process directly.
  • The consensus range is roughly 65–68°F (18–20°C): cool, not cold. This is the range sleep researchers most often cite.
  • Too warm is the bigger problem: heat causes restlessness, tossing, and less deep sleep. Being slightly cool is easier to fix with a blanket than being hot is to fix at all.
  • Warm hands and feet help you fall asleep faster: it sounds backwards, but dilating blood vessels in your extremities dumps core heat — that’s why warm socks can shorten the time to fall asleep.
  • A hot shower before bed works the same way: the post-shower drop in core temperature mimics (and speeds up) the natural pre-sleep cooldown.

Why Temperature Runs the Show

Sleep onset is tightly coupled to thermoregulation. In the evening, your body sheds heat through your hands, feet, and face, dropping core temperature — and that drop is one of the signals that initiates sleepiness. Anything that blocks heat loss (a hot room, heavy non-breathable bedding, a mattress that traps warmth) works against the process.

This is also why you tend to wake up when you’re too warm: as sleep lightens toward morning, your temperature regulation gets fussier, and overheating pulls you out of sleep earlier than you’d like.

The “Right” Temperature

The often-cited 65–68°F (18–20°C) range comes from sleep lab conditions and expert consensus — it’s cool enough to let your body dump heat, warm enough that you don’t lie there tense and shivering. Treat it as a starting point:

  • Hot sleepers: aim for the low end, or below. If you wake up sweaty, the room is too warm regardless of what the thermostat says.
  • Cold sleepers: the high end is fine. Being cold also disrupts sleep — shivering and muscle tension aren’t restful.
  • Couples who disagree: this is one of the most common bedroom conflicts. Practical fixes include separate blankets (Scandinavian style), breathable bedding for the hot sleeper, and a fan on one side.

Your body matters more than the number. If you sleep great at 70°F, don’t chase 65 because a guide told you to.

How to Actually Cool the Room

Ventilation first. An open window or a fan is the cheapest fix. Moving air feels several degrees cooler than still air, even at the same temperature.

Breathable bedding. Cotton, linen, and bamboo breathe; heavy synthetics and high-thread-count sateen trap heat. If you wake up damp, your bedding is a suspect before your thermostat is.

Mattress heat retention. Memory foam is notorious for sleeping warm — it softens with body heat and cradles you in it. If your mattress is the furnace, see our cooling mattress guide and our overview of mattress types, since some constructions sleep dramatically cooler than others.

The hot shower trick. A warm (not scalding) shower 1–2 hours before bed raises your skin temperature, which accelerates the subsequent core temperature drop. It’s one of the best-supported pre-sleep rituals in the literature.

Warm socks. Dilated blood vessels in warm feet dump core heat faster. It looks silly. It works.

What About Cold Rooms?

Genuinely cold bedrooms (below ~60°F/15°C) cause their own problems: muscle tension, restless shifting, and cold-induced wake-ups. The goal is cool, not cold. If your feet are ice blocks, add socks and a blanket before you touch the thermostat — extremities are easier to warm than the whole room.

The Honest Take

Of all the sleep environment tweaks — light, sound, bedding, gadgets — temperature has the strongest evidence and the lowest cost of fixing. Before buying anything for your sleep, spend a week noticing whether you wake up hot, cold, or comfortable. The answer tells you exactly where to start.

Cool room, warm feet, breathable covers. That’s most of sleep temperature science in nine words.
