Meedo vs. Premium Rooftop Tents: What's the Trade?

The Price Gap, Plainly Stated

  • Meedo Apollo A86 M (2-person): ~$999.99
  • Meedo Apollo TNT L (3-person): ~$1,499
  • Premium soft-shells (Roofnest, iKamper, etc.): ~$2,000–$4,000

Meedo costs roughly half. That ratio holds across the comparable soft-shell category — we’re comparing fold-out fabric tents to fold-out fabric tents, not soft-shells to hard-shells (hard-shells are a different product with faster setup and higher prices across all brands).

The question isn’t whether premium tents are better — they are, in specific ways. The question is whether those ways matter for how you’ll actually camp.

Spec-by-Spec: What the Numbers Say

Waterproofing: The Apollo TNT L lists a 4000mm waterproof rating. That’s a genuinely respectable number — for context, most quality camping gear considers 3000mm+ solid rain protection. Premium brands in this category typically list similar or somewhat higher ratings. For weekend rain, 4000mm is plenty; for multi-day deluges, higher ratings and better seam construction earn their keep.

Frame materials: Meedo lists aluminum-alloy frame components. Premium brands also use aluminum — this is one area where the spec sheets genuinely converge. Aluminum resists corrosion and keeps weight down versus steel, and both tiers have figured that out.

What’s included: Meedo includes the ladder in the box. Some premium brands do too; some sell it separately or integrate it differently. Always compare the out-the-door price, not the base price — a $2,500 tent that needs a $150 ladder is a $2,650 tent.

Mattress: Both tiers include a built-in foam mattress. Thickness and density vary by model on both sides — check the specific listings rather than assuming the premium tier is automatically thicker.

Manufacturing certification: Meedo’s parent company operates ISO 9001 and BSCI quality management systems. Premium Western brands typically cite their own QA processes and longer track records. Certification is reassuring; a decade of user reviews is more reassuring. This is where the premium tier’s real advantage lives — not in a single spec, but in thousands of nights of documented field use.

Where Premium Earns Its Price

Being honest about the trade means naming what the extra $1,000–$2,500 actually buys:

  1. Long-term durability data. Premium brands have years of user reports from full-time overlanders. Meedo has a smaller, newer user base. If you’re buying a tent to live in for six months, that data gap matters.
  2. Support and parts. US/EU-based support, readily available replacement parts, and established warranty processes. A broken zipper on a premium tent is a phone call; on a budget import, it’s a question mark.
  3. Refinement. Small things: better zippers, more thoughtful ventilation, cleaner stitching, easier rainfly handling. None is decisive alone; together they’re the “premium feel.”
  4. Resale value. Premium tents hold their value on the used market remarkably well. A $3,000 tent you sell for $2,000 cost you $1,000 net — suddenly the gap narrows.

That last point deserves emphasis: total cost of ownership can favor premium if you resell. But it only works if you actually resell, and most first-timers either keep the tent or let it gather dust.

Where Meedo Wins Outright

  1. Entry price. Half the cost to find out if you like rooftop camping. No contest.
  2. Weekend-grade specs. 4000mm waterproofing, aluminum frame, included ladder — the numbers that matter for 10–20 nights a year are all present.
  3. The opportunity cost. $1,500 saved is a season of trips: gas, park fees, a camp kitchen, better sleeping bags. Gear is a means; trips are the end.
  4. Direct sales. Meedo sells direct as the manufacturer’s consumer brand, which is structurally why the price is lower — fewer middlemen, not necessarily fewer materials.

Who Should Buy Which

Buy Meedo if: you camp a handful of weekends a year; this is your first rooftop tent and you’re still proving the concept; your budget is real and the savings fund actual trips; you can tolerate some uncertainty on long-term support.

Buy premium if: you’re planning extended overland travel (weeks to months); you camp 40+ nights a year and will genuinely wear a tent out; US-based support and parts availability are non-negotiable for you; you value resale and will actually sell when you upgrade.

Buy neither (yet) if: you don’t have a rated roof rack — the tent is only as safe as its mounting, and the rack is a separate $300–$600+ purchase that both tiers require.

The Bottom Line

The Meedo-vs-premium question resolves to usage, not quality absolutism. Premium tents earn their prices in durability data, support networks, refinement, and resale — real advantages for heavy users. Meedo matches the specs that matter for weekend camping at half the price — the rational choice for everyone else. Buy the tent that fits your actual camping life, not the one that fits your aspirations.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much cheaper is Meedo than premium rooftop tents? Roughly half. The Apollo A86 M runs ~$999.99 and the TNT L ~$1,499, versus ~$2,000–$4,000 for comparable soft-shells from premium brands like Roofnest or iKamper. Check the current price on Meedo’s site, since rooftop tent pricing shifts with freight costs.

Is a budget rooftop tent as waterproof as a premium one? On paper, close: the Apollo TNT L lists a 4000mm waterproof rating, which is solid rain protection comparable to much of the category. Where premium tents pull ahead is in sustained severe weather — multi-day deluges, where seam construction and higher ratings matter — and in years of documented field performance.

What do I give up buying Meedo instead of a premium brand? Mainly three things: long-term durability data (smaller, newer user base), US-based support and parts availability, and resale value (premium tents hold value well on the used market). For weekend campers these trade-offs rarely bite; for full-time overlanders they’re decisive.

Do I need anything besides the tent? Yes — a rated roof rack with crossbars, which both budget and premium tents require (typically $300–$600+ separately). Also check your vehicle’s dynamic roof weight rating and your garage clearance before ordering any rooftop tent.
